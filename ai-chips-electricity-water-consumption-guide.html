Which Chips Power AI? GPU, TPU, Electricity and Water Usage Explained

AI assistants, image generators and speech tools all depend on computing hardware. But which chips actually do that work? How much electricity does a GPU consume? And why do people say artificial intelligence uses water? This guide separates the figures manufacturers publish from the energy and water that real data centres consume.

In brief: NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, Google's TPUs, AWS Trainium and Inferentia, Intel Gaudi and phone/laptop NPUs can all accelerate AI. A processor's rated watts are not the energy used by an entire AI service. Water is consumed mainly through certain cooling systems and, indirectly, through electricity generation. There is no universal, scientifically defensible “litres of water per AI question” for every model, data centre and location.

1. What types of chips are useful for AI?

CPUGeneral-purpose computing: data preparation, control logic and small models
GPUMany parallel computations, including neural-network training and inference
TPU / custom acceleratorSpecialised tensor and matrix operations at high scale
NPU / edge AI chipEfficient on-device processing in phones, laptops and embedded systems

AI does not require one particular brand of processor. The right choice depends on the model's size, available memory, software ecosystem, latency and deployment scale. A CPU can train a small classifier; large language models commonly benefit from GPUs or specialised accelerators. More information: How silicon chips make AI computation possible.

Do not rank chips by watts alone. A 1,400 W accelerator could complete a certain compatible job faster than a 700 W accelerator and therefore use less energy per result; the reverse is also possible. Performance depends on model architecture, precision, memory capacity, throughput, utilisation and software. A fair efficiency comparison needs the same workload, accuracy and measurement boundary.

3. How much electricity does one AI chip consume?

Electricity usage is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), not watts. Watts describe power at a particular moment; kWh describes power multiplied by time. Manufacturer TDP or board-power values describe an engineering power envelope, not a guarantee of constant real-world consumption.

Energy (kWh) = Power (watts) ÷ 1,000 × Hours

To make the maths understandable, suppose a device actually drew its stated example power continuously for 24 hours:

A device can use less power at idle or under lighter jobs; dynamic utilisation and system configuration matter. The full facility also powers networking, storage, CPU hosts, cooling and electrical distribution.

4. Why does a data centre use more electricity than its AI chips?

Servers need electricity for processors, memory, storage, cooling fans and networking. The building additionally uses cooling infrastructure, power conversion, lighting and other systems. A common data-centre efficiency metric is Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE):

PUE = Total facility electricity ÷ IT equipment electricity

Illustrative facility example: If IT equipment uses 100 kW continuously and the facility's PUE is 1.30, total facility power is 130 kW. Over 24 hours, that equals 3,120 kWh for the entire facility and 2,400 kWh for IT alone. PUE = 1.30 is an assumed example, not a measurement from a particular provider.

Small differences in PUE, utilisation and energy supply matter greatly at large scale. PUE does not by itself report the water consumed or the carbon emitted.

5. How much electricity do AI and data centres use worldwide?

The International Energy Agency's 2026 update estimates that data centres globally consumed approximately 485 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity in 2025 and projects around 950 TWh in 2030 under its central scenario. It reports that total data-centre electricity demand grew by about 17% in 2025, while electricity use in AI-focused centres grew faster—around 50%.

These are all-data-centre figures. They include non-AI services such as storage, enterprise applications, web hosting, databases and other computing. The 2030 number is a projection, not a measured future fact. Local electricity grids can face much more concentrated pressure than the global percentage alone suggests.

For scale, 1 TWh = one billion kWh. The 485 TWh estimate therefore corresponds to 485 billion kWh across worldwide data centres in 2025. It cannot reliably be divided by a global number of “AI prompts” to calculate a universal prompt cost.

6. Why does AI use water?

Processors generate heat. Data centres must remove that heat to keep equipment within safe operating temperatures. Some cooling systems rely on evaporation or other processes that consume water; others use dry cooling or closed-loop circulation and can consume far less water onsite. The local climate, water source and cooling design strongly influence the result.

Direct water consumption

Water consumed at the data-centre site, often through evaporative cooling. Water withdrawn from a source and water actually consumed are different measurements.

Indirect water consumption

Water consumed upstream while generating the electricity used by the facility. The amount depends heavily on the electricity-generation mix and region.

There can also be water impacts from manufacturing semiconductors, including ultrapure water used in fabrication. That is a different life-cycle stage from daily operational cooling, and it should not be silently added to a cooling-only estimate.

A liquid-cooled rack is not automatically a water-wasting rack: some liquid-cooling designs circulate coolant in a closed loop. However, the system still needs to reject heat; water use depends on how that heat is ultimately discharged.

7. How much water do data centres actually consume? Published US figures

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's 2024 report on US data-centre energy estimates approximately 66 billion litres of direct, onsite water consumption in the United States during 2023. The same research estimates nearly 800 billion litres of indirect water consumption associated with electricity generation in 2023.

Both numbers cover US data centres as a group, including non-AI workloads; neither is a worldwide measurement or the amount spent to train one AI model. The indirect figure depends on power-system modelling and can differ under other generation assumptions. These scopes should not be presented as an exact water bill for ChatGPT, Gemini or any named AI service.

Why a single “water per prompt” number is misleading: An identical-looking request can trigger different model sizes, numbers of generated tokens, inference batches, computing locations, cooling technologies and electricity sources. Without workload-level measurement and transparent boundaries, a universal fixed number is unjustified.

8. How is water efficiency measured?

One commonly used metric is Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE), which measures annual site water usage relative to energy used by IT equipment. It is normally expressed as litres per kWh of IT energy.

WUE (L/kWh) = Site water usage (litres) ÷ IT electricity (kWh)

Illustrative calculation only: Assume a facility operates at 100 kW of IT load for 24 hours and reports WUE of 0.40 L/kWh. IT energy is 2,400 kWh and estimated onsite water consumption is 960 litres for the day (2,400 × 0.40). Here both the IT load and WUE are hypothetical; this is not an industry average or a figure for any specific AI service.

WUE covers onsite water under the chosen reporting boundary. It does not automatically include indirect electricity-generation water or semiconductor fabrication. The US Department of Energy explains the WUE concept in its federal data-centre cooling guidance.

10. Which AI chips should developers choose?

  • Learning AI on a laptop: Start with its CPU; many small experiments need no discrete GPU.
  • Running local models on a phone: Use supported mobile CPU/GPU/NPU features and compatible compact models.
  • Building neural-network applications: Select GPUs or other accelerators only when model size, memory and performance justify them.
  • Operating cloud AI services: Consider GPU services, Google TPUs, AWS Trainium/Inferentia and software compatibility, not just the wattage on a datasheet.
  • Reducing resource impact: Use appropriate model size, quantisation where quality permits, caching, batching, efficient serving and measurement of actual energy per useful result.

It is often more effective to make the software and model efficient before buying a much bigger processor. For a small website or learning project, avoid assuming a dedicated AI accelerator is necessary.

11. Conclusion

AI runs on many chip families: CPUs, NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, Google's TPUs, AWS Trainium and Inferentia, Intel's accelerators, and integrated NPUs. The choice depends on what the AI system must do. High power ratings indicate possible electrical demand under specified conditions, but they are not measured 24-hour energy use.

The environmental picture is bigger still. The electricity used by AI comes from a broader data-centre ecosystem, and water consumption involves cooling technology, local availability and electricity generation. Good AI engineering means measuring performance, electricity and water together—not treating a chip's headline watts as the whole story.

Frequently asked questions

Does every AI model need an NVIDIA chip?

No. AI can run on CPUs, AMD GPUs, Google TPUs, AWS-designed accelerators, Intel Gaudi and mobile NPUs, subject to software and model compatibility.

Does a 700 W GPU always consume 700 watts?

No. “Up to 700 W” is a rated configurable limit for certain versions, not a measurement of continuous power use. Actual draw depends on the workload and power configuration.

How much electricity will eight GPUs use in one day?

Eight chips each drawing exactly 700 W continuously would use 134.4 kWh in 24 hours for the chips alone. A real system also uses CPU, memory, networking and cooling electricity; actual draw varies.

How much water does one AI prompt use?

No universal fixed value applies. It depends on model and hardware, generated tokens, location, electricity mix and the definition of water consumption being used.

Does liquid cooling always waste large amounts of water?

No. Liquid cooling can use closed-loop coolant circulation, while ultimate heat rejection may use air, evaporation or other methods. The full site design determines onsite water consumption.

Is water used only to cool AI chips?

No. Water can also be consumed in generating electricity and manufacturing semiconductor chips, with each having a different measurement boundary.
