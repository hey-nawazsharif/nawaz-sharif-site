What Is Artificial Intelligence? The History and Evolution of AI

Artificial intelligence feels like a recent invention because chatbots and image generators are now widely available. But the story began decades before modern generative AI. It includes mathematical theories, early experiments, periods of disappointment and extraordinary progress in computing.

This beginner-friendly guide traces how artificial intelligence (AI) grew from an academic research idea into technology used in search, software, science and everyday work. You will learn which milestones mattered, what “AI winters” mean and why AI is much more than ChatGPT.

01 / The basics

What is artificial intelligence?

Artificial intelligence is the broad field of designing computer systems to perform tasks that involve abilities associated with intelligence, such as reasoning, interpreting information, understanding language, recognising patterns, planning and generating content.

Some systems follow hand-written rules and search through possible solutions. Others learn from examples using machine learning; many modern tools use deep learning, which relies on neural networks with multiple layers. These techniques overlap, but they are not identical.

Simple example: A rule-based IT assistant follows troubleshooting instructions. A machine-learning system predicts the category of a new helpdesk ticket. A generative AI assistant explains a solution in natural language. Each can help with a different part of the same workflow.

02 / Origins

Where did the idea of AI come from?

The ambition to create artificial reasoning has roots in philosophy, logic and mathematics. Electronic computers turned some of those ideas into experiments. In the 1940s, researchers examined mathematical models of computation and simplified artificial neurons. These models were far removed from modern deep neural networks, but they helped set the stage.

Alan Turing and the 1950 imitation game

In 1950, British mathematician Alan Turing published Computing Machinery and Intelligence. Instead of demanding an agreed definition of thinking, he proposed a conversation-based “imitation game” to discuss machine intelligence. Its influence remains important, but convincing conversation is not proof of consciousness or reliable reasoning.

The Dartmouth workshop in 1956

A 1955 research proposal by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon called for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College. Held in 1956, the workshop is widely regarded as the founding moment of AI as an organised research field. The name “artificial intelligence” appeared in the 1955 proposal.

What began as an ambitious research programme was not yet a practical general-purpose intelligent machine. Researchers still needed decades to develop algorithms, data resources and computing hardware.

03 / The milestones

A timeline of artificial intelligence history

These events highlight important turning points rather than every contribution. Different branches of AI developed in parallel.

  1. 1950

    Alan Turing examines machine intelligence

    His influential paper introduces the imitation game as a way to discuss whether machines can display intelligent behaviour.

  2. 1955–1956

    Artificial intelligence becomes a research field

    The Dartmouth proposal names AI and the 1956 workshop brings researchers together around the goal of machine intelligence.

  3. Late 1950s–1960s

    Early learning and symbolic programs

    Researchers experiment with perceptrons, logic-based problem solving and programs that manipulate language. Results are encouraging but limited to narrow tasks.

  4. 1970s

    First widely recognised AI winter

    Hardware limitations, fragile results and expectations beyond the technology's capabilities contribute to reduced enthusiasm and funding.

  5. 1980s

    Expert systems expand

    Knowledge-based systems apply specialist rules to specific business and technical tasks, but can be expensive to maintain.

  6. Late 1980s–early 1990s

    Another AI winter

    Expert-system limitations and a downturn in specialised AI computing contribute to another period of weaker investment and confidence.

  7. 1997

    IBM Deep Blue defeats Garry Kasparov

    A purpose-built chess system defeats the reigning world champion in a match. This shows the strength of specialised search and computation, not human-level general intelligence.

  8. 2012

    AlexNet reshapes image recognition

    A deep convolutional neural network wins the ImageNet image classification competition, helping accelerate interest in deep learning and GPU-based training.

  9. 2016

    AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol

    DeepMind's system combines neural networks and search with reinforcement-learning methods to achieve a landmark result in the game of Go.

  10. 2017

    The Transformer architecture is introduced

    The paper Attention Is All You Need introduces an attention-based neural-network architecture that becomes central to many later language models.

  11. November 30, 2022

    ChatGPT is released to the public

    OpenAI introduces ChatGPT, bringing conversational generative AI to a far broader audience.

  12. 2023–2026

    Multimodal and tool-connected AI grows

    AI systems increasingly work with text, images, audio, programming and integrated workflows. Reliability, safety, privacy and cost continue to matter.

Key takeaway: AI did not start in 2022. ChatGPT is one modern milestone in a much longer history of research, disappointment and progress.
04 / Setbacks

What were the AI winters?

An AI winter is a period when excitement or investment falls after results fail to meet high expectations. Historians differ on precise boundaries, but the mid-to-late 1970s and the late 1980s into the early 1990s are the two periods most often highlighted.

Early systems could perform impressive demonstrations but struggled with real-world ambiguity. Computing power and data were limited. Later expert systems relied on large collections of rules that were hard to maintain when conditions changed. Many researchers continued important work even as commercial enthusiasm cooled.

The lesson is not that AI failed. It is that real-world capabilities advance through testing and engineering, not headlines alone.

05 / Technical evolution

From rule-based AI to generative AI

Symbolic AI

Systems represent knowledge through symbols, logical rules and search. They are useful for well-defined problems but can struggle with exceptions.

Statistical machine learning

Models learn patterns from datasets, supporting tasks such as predictions, classifications and anomaly detection.

Deep learning

Multi-layer neural networks learn complex representations of images, speech, language and other data, often needing substantial computation.

Generative AI

Generative models produce content such as text, code, images and audio. Many modern models use deep learning and the Transformer architecture.

Did deep learning replace older AI?

No. A useful product might combine predictable rules, a traditional machine-learning model and a language model. “More advanced” does not automatically mean better for the task.

06 / Everyday use

Where do we see AI today?

IT and software development

AI can help classify support tickets, search technical documents, draft code and assist troubleshooting. Changes to important systems still require verification and authorisation.

Cybersecurity

Monitoring can combine rules and machine learning to prioritise unusual events. An anomaly is a signal for investigation, not proof of a cyberattack. Read our anomaly detection tutorial in Python for a safe synthetic-data exercise.

Search, accessibility and science

Machine learning helps rank results and personalise services. AI research also supports transcription, accessibility tools and scientific data analysis. High-stakes applications need representative testing and human oversight.

07 / Responsible progress

What can the history of AI teach us?

  • Fluent is not always correct. Generative systems can produce persuasive but inaccurate answers.
  • Training data matters. Bias, missing information and changes in real-world conditions can reduce reliability.
  • Privacy and security matter. Do not submit confidential data to services without the proper permissions and safeguards.
  • AI costs vary. Large models may require significant energy and computing infrastructure; simpler tools can be more suitable.
  • Human review remains essential. Especially for health, financial, employment and cybersecurity decisions.

AI's past is a reminder that meaningful demonstrations and trustworthy deployment are not the same thing. Good results must be measured against real user needs and clear limitations.

08 / Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Who invented artificial intelligence?

No single person invented the entire field. John McCarthy helped name AI, and Alan Turing, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, Nathaniel Rochester and many others contributed foundational ideas.

When was the phrase artificial intelligence first used?

It appeared in the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, held in 1956.

Is ChatGPT the first AI system?

No. AI research dates back decades. ChatGPT was introduced publicly in 2022.

Why was 2012 important?

AlexNet's ImageNet result helped demonstrate the practical effectiveness of deep neural networks trained with large datasets and modern hardware.

Does AI mean human-level intelligence?

No. Most systems are designed or evaluated for particular capabilities. A convincing answer or strong result in one domain is not proof of general human-like intelligence.
